# Application answers

## Two files, and they are not interchangeable

This skill covers two artifacts. They read alike and they live apart.

- **`cover-letter.md`** — a cover letter, a "why this company" note, an
  introduction, anything you are writing because it would help rather than
  because it was asked for. If in doubt, it is this one.
- **`application-answers.md`** — answers to questions the employer actually
  asked, written beneath each question, in the file that already holds them.

That file only exists because the user pasted the employer's questions into it.
If it is not there, no questions were asked, and writing a cover letter into it
puts the letter somewhere nobody will look for it. Never create it.

## Research before writing a word

Find what this company actually screens for. Recruiter interviews, hiring
guides, founder posts about culture. Specific signals, not "show passion". Then
map the candidate's real experience onto those signals.

## The swap test

Replace the company name with a direct competitor. Does the answer still work?

If yes, it is not an answer, it is a template. Rewrite until it contains
something **only true about this company**, framed through something the
candidate has actually done.

## While writing

- Do not recite the company's achievements back at them. They know. Frame their
  decisions through what the candidate has learned from their own work.
- No Hollywood lines. Write like a smart person typing an important email.
- Run the anti-AI check *during* writing, not after. See the `writing-voice`
  skill. Writing model-shaped prose and sanding it down afterwards leaves the
  shape behind.

## Prose someone reads once

A CV is skimmed and a prep document is scanned, so both survive being dense.
This is neither. A reply to a recruiter, a cover letter, a free-text answer:
somebody reads it top to bottom on a phone, once, and forms a view. Density that
works on a page fails here.

- **Paragraph breaks, every three or four sentences.** A four-hundred-word block
  is not read, it is weighed. This is the single commonest failure and it turns
  strong material into something that looks like output.
- **Cut the weakest third.** The instinct is to include every good thing, and
  the effect is that the best thing is now one item in a list. Whatever is
  third-best is costing you the top two.
- **Never open with velocity.** "Forty commits", "eighty percent of the repo",
  "twelve people". Those are answers to how much, and nobody has asked how much;
  they have asked what you built and whether you understood it. A figure earns
  its place mid-paragraph as evidence for a claim, not as the claim.
- **One figure only survives alone.** Before quoting a number, read it with no
  sentence around it. "First commit to App Store in 45 days" means something by
  itself. "28% of merged commits" invites a question you would rather not spend
  the reply answering.
- **Say which part you are proudest of, and why.** Every candidate lists what
  they did. Almost none says which part mattered, and that sentence is usually
  the one that gets remembered.
- **Land the lesson on their business.** A hard-won lesson is interesting; the
  same lesson pointed at the specific way their product could fail is the thing
  that gets a reply.
- **No sign-off flourish.** "Looking forward to hearing from you", "excited by
  what you are building". Stop at the last real sentence.

## Answering a direct question

When an employer asks something specific — what have you built, tell us about a
time — answer the question asked, in the order they asked it, and only then
volunteer anything else. A reply that opens with what the candidate wanted to
say rather than what was asked reads as a prepared statement.

If they list what they want covered (architecture, trade-offs, lessons), each
one gets a home. Not a heading each: a sentence each, in the flow.

## Process

Research first, write once. One draft, not nine. Use subagents for research, not
for producing competing drafts to pick between: that produces an average, and an
average cover letter is a rejected one.
